Starlink's disaster playbook: free service after Hurricane Lowell and the Colombia quake, and where Tesla fits in

Starlink · October 23, 2026

Starlink offered free service after Hurricane Lowell and the Colombia earthquake, and now has a disaster deal with the US State Department. How it works, and how Cybertrucks and Tesla batteries have joined in.

When a hurricane or earthquake knocks out phone lines and mobile masts, Starlink has become one of the first tools emergency teams reach for. This autumn alone, SpaceX offered free service after Hurricane Lowell hit the Hawaiian island of Kauai and after a major earthquake in Colombia. Tesla has repeatedly been part of the same picture, with Cybertrucks carrying Starlink dishes and battery systems keeping the lights on. Here is how the playbook works.

Kauai and Colombia: the latest examples

  • Hurricane Lowell, Kauai: on September 10, 2026, Starlink announced free service through October 11 for every new and existing customer on the island. More than 200 Starlink kits were handed to emergency organisations across Kauai, separate from the household offer.
  • Earthquake, Colombia: after a magnitude 7.4 quake struck western Colombia on August 10, killing at least 250 people according to reports at the time, Starlink offered free service in affected areas through September 12. Existing customers received an automatic credit, former customers could reactivate for free, and damaged dishes were eligible for free replacement.

A formal role with the US government

In June 2026, Starlink signed a two-year memorandum of understanding with the US State Department to restore communications after natural disasters worldwide. The department's disaster response bureau coordinates with Starlink to connect rescue workers, aid groups and affected people. The agreement builds on Starlink's own crisis response work, which supported relief efforts in Indonesia, the Philippines and Sri Lanka in the months before.

Where Tesla fits in

Tesla has turned up alongside Starlink in several emergencies:

  • Los Angeles wildfires, January 2025: Tesla borrowed Cybertrucks that were due to be delivered to customers, fitted them with Starlink dishes and parked them in affected areas to provide free WiFi. According to Tesla, trucks went to Zuma Beach and Malibu, Pasadena, and Altadena, where three were placed with the LA Sheriff's Department. Customers whose deliveries were delayed were promised replacements by the end of that week.
  • Hurricane Helene, 2024: Tesla sent mobile power units to North Carolina, while Starlink shipped 140 kits to the state within a week of the storm.
  • Puerto Rico, 2017: after Hurricane Maria, Tesla sent Powerwall batteries to the island.

The logic is simple. A Starlink dish needs electricity, and after a disaster the grid is often down too. A Tesla vehicle with a large battery, a mobile power unit or a Powerwall can run a dish for long periods. Together they form a self-contained internet hotspot that does not depend on local infrastructure.

Why it matters

For Starlink, disaster response builds goodwill and shows governments what the network can do, which helps in markets where it is still waiting for approval. For Tesla, it shows a use for its batteries and trucks beyond daily driving. It is also a reminder of how intertwined Musk's companies are in practice: the same disaster response can involve SpaceX satellites and Tesla hardware, even though the two firms are formally separate and only now studying a merger.

There are limits. Free service usually runs for a month or so, and new customers still need hardware. Satellite capacity in a small area is finite. And charity by a commercial company is no substitute for public emergency planning.

What to watch

How Starlink responds to the rest of the 2026 hurricane season, whether the State Department agreement leads to more coordinated deployments, and whether Tesla and SpaceX make their joint emergency kits a formal offering rather than an improvised one.
