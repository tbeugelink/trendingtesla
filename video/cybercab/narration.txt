On September twenty second, twenty twenty, at Battery Day, Musk stood in front of a white vehicle render. Three years from that night was twenty twenty three. The car did not arrive. Commentators hung the name Model 2 on it. Tesla never did.

On April fifth, twenty twenty four, Reuters reported that Tesla had canceled the long-promised inexpensive car, and would keep developing self-driving robotaxis on the same small-vehicle platform. Musk posted that Reuters was lying, again. He did not name the line.

Eighteen days later, Tesla's update filed on April twenty third told shareholders the lineup had changed. New vehicles, including more affordable models, would use aspects of the next generation platform and aspects of the current platforms, and would be produced on the same manufacturing lines as the current lineup. Tesla said that update might mean less cost reduction than previously expected.

The third-quarter twenty twenty four update kept those more affordable models on track to begin launching in the first half of twenty twenty five. It is also the update that says, in October, Tesla unveiled Cybercab and Robovan, both designed from the ground up for autonomy, without a steering wheel or pedals.

The fourth-quarter update separated the two ideas. More affordable models stayed on track for the start of production in the first half of twenty twenty five, on the same lines, with less cost reduction than expected. The purpose-built robotaxi product, Cybercab, would continue the unboxed manufacturing strategy, and was scheduled for volume production starting in twenty twenty six. Preparation was underway for Cybercab lines at Gigafactory Texas.

In the first quarter of twenty twenty five, Tesla wrote that more affordable options were as critical as ever, and that the Cybercab lines in Texas remained on track for volume production the next year. Next year, from that letter, is twenty twenty six.
